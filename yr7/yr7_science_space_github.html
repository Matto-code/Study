What this unit is about

The big question from slide 2: how does our world fit into everything? The unit zooms out from Earth to the Solar System, to the stars and galaxies, then zooms back in to explain things we notice every day: day and night, time zones, the seasons, the Moon's phases, eclipses and tides.

The one idea that explains most of the exam

Almost everything in this unit comes from three movements: Earth spinning on its tilted axis (day and night, time zones), Earth orbiting the Sun (years and seasons), and the Moon orbiting Earth (phases, eclipses, tides). Gravity is what holds all of it together.

The three movements

MovementTakesCauses
Earth rotates (spins) on its axis24 hoursDay and night, the Sun "rising" and "setting", time zones
Earth revolves (orbits) around the Sun365¼ daysOne year. With the 23.4° tilt, the seasons
Moon orbits Earthabout 29.5 days (new moon to new moon)Moon phases, eclipses, and (with gravity) tides
Rotate vs revolve

Rotate = spin on the spot (like a figure skater). Revolve = travel around something else (like running laps). Exams love mixing these up.

Inner planets

Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars. Small, rocky, close to the Sun, few or no moons, no rings.

Outer planets

Jupiter, Saturn (gas giants), Uranus, Neptune (ice giants). Huge, mostly gas, far away, lots of moons, all have rings.

Patterns to spot

The further a planet is from the Sun, the longer its year and (mostly) the colder it is. Venus breaks the temperature pattern: its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere traps heat, so it's hotter than Mercury.

The Sun Slide 17

  • A star, and the largest object in the Solar System. About 1.3 million Earths would fit inside it.
  • Its gravity holds the Solar System together.
  • Made mostly of hydrogen and helium.
  • Hottest part is the core: about 15 million °C. The surface is about 5500 °C.
  • Light from the Sun takes about 8 minutes to reach Earth.

Dwarf planets and the Kuiper Belt Slides 19–20

In 2006 scientists agreed that a planet must pass three tests:

  1. It orbits the Sun.
  2. It has enough mass (gravity) to pull itself into a round shape.
  3. It has cleared its orbit (it's the dominant object on its path).

A dwarf planet passes tests 1 and 2 but fails test 3. That's why Pluto was reclassified: it shares its orbit with lots of other icy objects in the Kuiper Belt.

Our 5 dwarf planets

Pluto, Ceres, Eris, Haumea, Makemake. Ceres is in the asteroid belt (between Mars and Jupiter). The other four are out past Neptune.

The Kuiper Belt is a huge ring of icy objects beyond Neptune. Pluto is part of it, along with many other dwarf planets.

Exoplanets Slides 21–22

An exoplanet is a planet outside our Solar System. Most orbit other stars; a few float freely through space. More than 6000 have been confirmed so far.

So why isn't Pluto an exoplanet?

Because Pluto orbits our Sun, so it's inside our Solar System. "Exo" means outside. Pluto is a dwarf planet, not an exoplanet.

Don't mix these up

Slide 21 lists "not enough mass to be spherical" and "hasn't cleared its orbit" under exoplanets. Those two points are really about why something is not a full planet (and the "cleared its orbit" one is the dwarf planet test). If an exam asks what an exoplanet is, the answer is simply: a planet that orbits a star other than our Sun.

How our model of the Solar System changed Slides 13–16

Geocentric model

Geo = Earth. Earth in the centre, with the Sun, Moon, planets and stars going around it. Believed by the ancient Greeks.

Heliocentric model

Helio = Sun. The Sun in the centre, with Earth and the other planets orbiting it. This is the correct model.

Why did the Greeks think Earth was in the middle?

From where they stood, everything in the sky appeared to move around them: the Sun crosses the sky each day, and the stars wheel around at night. They couldn't feel Earth moving, so it made sense to think Earth was still and everything else moved.

Timeline

Ancient Greece

Geocentric model. Earth at the centre.

1543

Copernicus publishes a book putting the Sun at the centre. Earth rotates on its axis and revolves around the Sun. More correct, but it couldn't explain all the planets' movements (he used perfect circles).

1610

Galileo uses a telescope. He sees four moons orbiting Jupiter (proof that not everything orbits Earth) and that Venus has phases like our Moon (which only makes sense if Venus orbits the Sun).

1600s

Kepler shows orbits are ellipses (ovals), not circles. This fixed the problems in Copernicus's model.

Why Galileo got in trouble

The Church taught the geocentric model. Supporting the heliocentric model meant Galileo was questioning the Church's authority. This is a good example of how science ideas change when new evidence (from new technology like the telescope) comes along.

Gravity Slides 18, 60

Definition

Gravity is an invisible force of attraction between any objects that have mass.

  • More mass = stronger pull. The Sun has by far the most mass, so everything in the Solar System orbits it.
  • More distance = weaker pull.
  • The particles in a planet are all pulled toward its centre. That's why big objects (planets, stars, dwarf planets) end up round.
  • Gravity pulls both ways: Earth pulls on the Moon and the Moon pulls on Earth (this causes tides).

Distances to the stars Slides 23–28

Stars are so far apart that kilometres become useless numbers. Astronomers use the light-year.

Definition

A light-year is the distance light travels in a vacuum in one year. It's about 9.5 trillion km.

Trap

A light-year measures distance, not time, even though it has "year" in the name.

  • Speed of light: c = 299 792 458 m/s (about 300 000 km every second).
  • The closest star system to the Sun is Alpha Centauri, 4.37 light-years away. Through a telescope it splits into a double star.
  • When you look at Alpha Centauri, you see light that left it over 4 years ago. Looking far away means looking back in time.

Parallax Slides 29–31

Parallax is the way a nearby object seems to change position against a distant background when you look at it from two different places.

  • As Earth orbits, astronomers observe a star from two positions 6 months apart (opposite sides of the orbit, about 300 million km apart).
  • They measure how far the star appears to shift.
  • Bigger shift = closer star. Smaller shift = further away.
  • Even the nearest stars shift by a tiny amount, so this measurement is very hard to do.
Try it (this was the prac)

Hold your thumb up at arm's length. Close one eye, then swap eyes. Your thumb "jumps" against the background. Now bring your thumb closer to your face and repeat: it jumps more. Closer objects show more parallax.

What else is out there? Slides 32–33

  • A galaxy is a huge collection of stars, gas and dust held together by gravity.
  • Our galaxy is the Milky Way, a spiral galaxy about 100 000 light-years across. Our Solar System is in one of its arms, not the centre.
  • There are about 2 trillion galaxies (2 000 000 000 000).
  • There are about 2 septillion stars (a 2 followed by 24 zeros).

Properties of stars Slides 34–35

Astronomers classify stars by colour, size and brightness. Other properties are chemical composition and mass.

Colour tells you temperature

A star's colour shows the temperature of its surface. Blue = hottest. Red = coolest.

Memory hook

It's the opposite of taps. On a tap, blue means cold and red means hot. For stars, blue is hottest (think of the blue flame on a gas stove, which is hotter than a yellow flame).

Earth's rotation and day/night Slides 36–37

  • Earth spins on an axis, an imaginary line from the North Pole to the South Pole.
  • The axis is tilted about 23.4° compared with Earth's orbit.
  • One full rotation takes 24 hours = one day.
  • The half of Earth facing the Sun has day; the half facing away has night.
  • The change from day to night is gradual and continuous: every place slowly moves from light into darkness (dawn and dusk).
  • Earth spins from west to east, which is why the Sun rises in the east and sets in the west.
Common mistake

Day and night are caused by Earth spinning (rotation), not by Earth going around the Sun.

Time zones Slides 39–42

Because Earth rotates, it's daytime in some places while it's night in others. Time zones let every place set its clocks so that midday is roughly when the Sun is highest.

  • Earth turns 360° in 24 hours = 15° every hour, so there are about 24 time zones.
  • Places to the east see the Sun first, so they are ahead. Places to the west are behind.
  • Times are measured from UTC (the time in London in winter). Brisbane is UTC+10.
  • The International Date Line runs through the Pacific Ocean. Cross it going west and you skip forward a day.
Method

1. Find the difference: other place's UTC number − Brisbane's (+10).
2. Add that many hours to the Brisbane time (a negative number means go back).
3. Check whether you've crossed midnight. If so, change the day.

Worked example: 10 am Friday in Brisbane → Brazil (UTC−3)

Difference: −3 − 10 = −13 hours.

10 am − 13 hours: 10 am − 10 h = midnight, then −3 more = 9 pm.

We went back past midnight, so it's the day before: 9 pm Thursday.

Why do we have seasons? Slides 43–48

The answer in one sentence

Seasons happen because Earth's axis is tilted (23.4°), so as Earth orbits the Sun, each hemisphere takes turns being tilted toward the Sun (summer) and away from the Sun (winter).

When your hemisphere is tilted toward the Sun:

  • Sunlight hits more directly (higher in the sky), so its heat is more concentrated.
  • Days are longer, so there are more hours of heating.
MonthEarth's positionAustralia (South)North America (North)

The two hemispheres always have opposite seasons. Seasons flow smoothly from one to the next; there's no sharp boundary between them.

The big misconception

Seasons are NOT caused by Earth being closer to or further from the Sun. Proof: Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January, which is winter in the Northern Hemisphere. If distance caused seasons, both hemispheres would have summer at the same time.

Longest and shortest days Slides 49–51

Solstice

When a hemisphere is tilted most toward or most away from the Sun. The longest day (summer solstice) or shortest day (winter solstice). For Australia: about 22 December (longest) and 21 June (shortest).

Equinox

In autumn and spring, when day and night are both about 12 hours everywhere. For Australia: about 20 March and 23 September.

  • The difference between summer and winter day length is biggest at the poles and smallest near the equator.
  • Near the poles in summer the Sun can stay up for 24 hours; in winter it can stay dark for 24 hours.
  • In Brisbane the longest day is about 13 h 50 min and the shortest is about 10 h 25 min.
Watch the hemisphere

The diagram on slide 51 is drawn for the Northern Hemisphere, so its "summer solstice" is in June. In Australia everything is flipped: June is our winter solstice.

Phases of the Moon Slides 55–56

  • The Moon makes no light of its own. It reflects sunlight.
  • The Sun always lights up half of the Moon. As the Moon orbits Earth, we see different amounts of that lit half. These are the phases.
  • A full cycle (new moon to new moon) takes about 29.5 days.
  • Waxing = the lit part is growing. Waning = the lit part is shrinking.

Southern Hemisphere trick

In Australia the Moon spells C for Coming (waxing, lit on the left) and D for Departing (waning, lit on the right). In the Northern Hemisphere it's the other way round, which is why slide 56 says phases look different depending on your hemisphere.

Common mistake

Phases are not caused by Earth's shadow falling on the Moon. They're caused by how much of the Moon's sunlit half we can see from Earth. Earth's shadow on the Moon is a lunar eclipse, which is rare.

Eclipses Slides 57–58

Solar eclipse

The Sun is blocked out by the Moon.
Order: Sun – Moon – Earth.
Can only happen at a new moon. The Moon's shadow lands on a small part of Earth, so only people in that path see it. Never look straight at the Sun.

Lunar eclipse

The Moon is blocked out by Earth (Earth's shadow falls on the Moon).
Order: Sun – Earth – Moon.
Can only happen at a full moon. Everyone on the night side of Earth can see it. The Moon often turns red ("blood moon"). Safe to watch.

If eclipses need a new or full moon, why don't we get two every month?

The Moon's orbit is tilted slightly (about 5°) compared with Earth's orbit. Most months the Moon passes a little above or below the Sun–Earth line, so the shadows miss. An eclipse only happens when the three line up exactly.

New moon vs lunar eclipse (slide 57)

A new moon happens every month: the Moon is between Earth and Sun and we can't see its lit side. A lunar eclipse is a rare event where Earth blocks sunlight from reaching a full moon.

Coming up: a total solar eclipse will cross Australia on 22 July 2028, passing over Sydney.

Tides Slides 60–62

  • The Moon's gravity pulls on Earth's oceans, making water bulge toward the Moon (high tide).
  • There's also a bulge on the opposite side of Earth. Earth itself is pulled toward the Moon, leaving that water behind.
  • The places in between (at right angles to the Moon) have water drawn away: low tide.
  • As Earth spins through the two bulges, most beaches get 2 high tides and 2 low tides each day.

Spring tide

Sun and Moon line up with Earth (new moon and full moon). Their pulls add together: higher high tides and lower low tides.

Neap tide

Sun and Moon are at right angles (first and last quarter). The Sun's pull works against the Moon's: lower high tides and higher low tides.

Trap

"Spring tide" has nothing to do with the season spring. It happens twice a month, all year round. Think of the water "springing up".

Meteor words: follow the rock

Meteoroid in space → meteor in the sky (burning up from friction with the air) → meteorite on the ground. Slide 63 calls them all "meteorites", but this is the exact difference the "Less Than Five" video explains.

  • Halley's Comet comes back every 76 years. It was last seen in 1986 and returns in 2061.
  • A comet's tail always points away from the Sun, because it's pushed by sunlight and the solar wind.
  • About 66 million years ago an asteroid about 10 km wide hit Earth, which is linked to the dinosaurs dying out. Scientists now track near-Earth asteroids to spot dangerous ones early.
  • The Moon is covered in craters because it has no air to burn up incoming rocks and no weather to wear the craters away.

Data analysis checklist (what the exam will ask)

  • Average: add the trials and divide by how many. (38 + 40 + 39) ÷ 3 = 39.
  • Why repeat trials? To make the results more reliable and spot mistakes (outliers).
  • Graph: independent variable (height) on the x-axis, dependent (crater diameter) on the y-axis. Label both axes with units. Use a line or scatter graph because both variables are numbers.
  • Trend: "As the drop height increased, the crater diameter increased."
  • Explain: a higher drop means the ball is going faster when it hits, so it has more energy to push sand out of the way.
  • Link to space: faster or bigger meteorites make bigger craters.
