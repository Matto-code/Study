🗂️ Rows and columns mean something

The periodic table isn't just a random grid — its layout directly reflects atomic structure.

Rows (Periods)
Tell you how many electron shells (energy levels) an atom uses. Period 3 elements all use 3 shells.
Columns (Groups)
Tell you how many valence electrons (outer shell electrons) an atom has. This is what drives chemical behaviour.

This is why elements in the same column behave similarly — they all have the same number of outer electrons, so they bond in similar ways.

📍 Where metals and non-metals sit

Draw a rough diagonal "staircase" line from boron down to astatine — everything to the left of it is a metal, everything to the right is a non-metal, and the elements sitting right along the line are metalloids.

  • Metals — the majority of the table, all main groups on the left plus every transition metal in the middle block.
  • Non-metals — top-right corner (plus hydrogen, an exception sitting up on its own).
  • Metalloids — a thin diagonal strip between the two (e.g. silicon, germanium, arsenic).

⚡ What are valence electrons?

Valence electrons are the electrons in an atom's outermost shell. They're the only electrons involved in bonding — which makes them the reason atoms react the way they do.

Quick rule: for main group elements, the number of valence electrons matches the group number (drop the leading "1" for groups 13–18). Group 16 → 6 valence electrons. Group 1 → 1 valence electron.

➕➖ Cations and anions

Atoms react to get a full outer shell, like the stable noble gases. Depending on how many valence electrons they start with, they'll either lose or gain electrons to get there.

Cation — a positive ion, formed when an atom loses electrons (protons now outnumber electrons). e.g. Mg → Mg²⁺
Anion — a negative ion, formed when an atom gains electrons (electrons now outnumber protons). e.g. F → F⁻

🔀 Transition metals: variable charge

Metals in the middle block (groups 3–12) don't follow the simple group-number rule — they can form more than one possible ion, depending on what they're bonding with. This is shown using Roman numerals in the metal's name.

MetalPossible ionsNamed as
Iron (Fe)Fe²⁺ or Fe³⁺Iron(II) or Iron(III)
Copper (Cu)Cu⁺ or Cu²⁺Copper(I) or Copper(II)
Exam tip: the Roman numeral is the charge on the metal ion in that particular compound — you often have to work it out backwards from the charge of whatever it's bonded to.

✍️ Naming rule

Ionic compounds form between a metal (cation) and a non-metal (anion). Naming follows a simple pattern:

Metal name (unchanged) + non-metal name (ending changed to "-ide")
CationAnionCompoundName
Na⁺Cl⁻NaClSodium chloride
Mg²⁺O²⁻MgOMagnesium oxide
Al³⁺N³⁻AlNAluminium nitride
Order matters: the metal (cation) is always written and named first; the non-metal (anion) is always written and named last.

⚖️ Balancing the charges — cross-multiplication

The overall compound must be electrically neutral, so positive and negative charges must balance out exactly. The cross-multiplication method makes this quick:

  1. Write both ions with their charges.
  2. Cross the number of each charge over to become the other ion's subscript.
  3. Simplify if both subscripts share a common factor.
  4. Never write a subscript of 1 — it's left blank.

Worked examples

Mg²⁺  Cl⁻
MgCl₂

Charge 2 from Mg crosses to become Cl's subscript → 1 Mg, 2 Cl

Ca²⁺  N³⁻
Ca₃N₂

Charges 2 and 3 cross over → 3 Ca, 2 N (no common factor to simplify)

Fe³⁺  O²⁻
Fe₂O₃
Watch out: Na⁺ and O²⁻ cross-multiplies to Na₂O₁ — but since we never write a subscript of 1, this simplifies to Na₂O. Don't confuse this with "2NaO", which means something completely different (2 separate NaO units vs. one Na₂O molecule).

🧪 The two polyatomic exceptions

At this stage you only need two polyatomic ions (ions made of more than one atom, acting as a single unit) — everything else stays as simple single-element ions:

Polyatomic ionFormulaCharge
HydroxideOH−1
CarbonateCO₃−2

These are treated as a single block when balancing charges. If you need more than one of a polyatomic ion, put it in brackets with the subscript outside:

Mg²⁺ + OH⁻ → Mg(OH)₂

Brackets show 2 whole hydroxide ions, not 2 separate oxygens.

CaCO₃ — Calcium carbonate

1 calcium atom, 1 carbon atom, 3 oxygen atoms — the "carbonate" name doesn't change even though it's polyatomic.

⚖️ The Law of Conservation of Mass

In any chemical reaction, atoms are rearranged — never created or destroyed. That means the total mass of everything you start with must equal the total mass of everything you end up with.

Law of Conservation of Mass: in a closed system, the total mass of reactants equals the total mass of products.

🔓 Closed systems vs open systems

Closed system
Nothing can enter or leave — all reactants and products (including any gas produced) stay inside and get weighed. Mass will always balance.
Open system
Something can escape (usually a gas). If you only weigh what's left behind, it looks like mass has "disappeared" — but it hasn't; it just left the container.

🧪 The practical: sodium bicarbonate + vinegar

This reaction produces carbon dioxide gas — a great way to test conservation of mass, because that gas can either escape (open system) or be trapped (closed system).

sodium bicarbonate + vinegar → sodium acetate + water + carbon dioxide

Method

  1. Weigh the flask, sodium bicarbonate, and vinegar separately before mixing.
  2. Add the bicarbonate to the flask, then add the vinegar. Let it react, then weigh the whole system.
  3. Repeat the reaction, but this time stretch a balloon over the top of the flask before it reacts, trapping any gas produced. Weigh the whole system again afterwards.

What happens

SetupResultWhy
No balloon (open system)Mass appears to decreaseCO₂ gas escapes into the air and isn't weighed
With balloon (closed system)Mass stays the sameCO₂ gas is trapped by the balloon, so nothing actually leaves the system
Key takeaway: mass is never actually lost in either case — the open system just looks like it lost mass because a product escaped before it could be weighed. This is exactly why the "closed system" condition matters for the law.

🧮 Working out total mass

Total mass BEFORE = mass of flask + mass of vinegar + mass of sodium bicarbonate
Total mass AFTER = mass shown on the scale (with everything, including any balloon, still in place)

If total mass before ≈ total mass after, conservation of mass is confirmed. Any big difference points to something escaping the system rather than the law being "wrong."
