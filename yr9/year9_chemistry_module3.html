🔄 Physical vs chemical change

Not every change to a substance is a chemical reaction. Telling the two apart is one of the most useful skills in chemistry.

Physical change
No new substance is formed. The material's chemical makeup stays the same — usually just its state, shape, or size changes. Often reversible. e.g. melting ice, dissolving sugar, tearing paper.
Chemical change
A new substance is formed with different properties. The atoms have rearranged into something new. Usually difficult to reverse. e.g. burning wood, rusting iron, baking a cake.

🔍 Evidence of a chemical reaction

You can't always see atoms rearranging, but there are physical clues that a chemical change has probably happened:

Colour change
Gas produced (bubbles)
Precipitate forms
Temperature change
Light or sound produced
New smell released
Careful: a temperature or state change alone isn't proof — ice melting gets warmer with no chemical change at all. Look for multiple clues together, or check if the new substance can be un-made by simply reversing the physical conditions.

✍️ Parts of a chemical equation

A chemical equation describes a chemical change using the substances involved:

Reactant(s) → Product(s)
  • Reactant — the chemical(s) you start with, before the reaction. Written on the left.
  • Product — the new chemical(s) formed by the reaction. Written on the right.
  • Reaction symbol (→) — read as "produces" or "yields".

Word equation example

nitrogen + hydrogen → ammonia

⚖️ Rules for balancing equations

  1. Matter cannot be created or destroyed — atoms must match on both sides.
  2. Subscripts cannot be added, removed, or changed — they're fixed by the chemical formula.
  3. You can only change coefficients (the numbers in front).
  4. Coefficients only go in front of a chemical formula — never in the middle.
Extra tips: balance the biggest/most complex formulas first and save single (free) elements for last. If the same polyatomic ion appears unchanged on both sides, treat it as one unit rather than splitting it into separate atoms.

🧪 Worked example: burning methane

Here's the unbalanced equation for methane combustion — notice the atom counts don't match:

CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

Reactant side

  • 1 carbon atom
  • 4 hydrogen atoms
  • 2 oxygen atoms

Product side

  • 1 carbon atom
  • 2 hydrogen atoms
  • 3 oxygen atoms

Hydrogen and oxygen don't match — not balanced yet.

Adding a coefficient of 2 in front of both O₂ and H₂O fixes it:

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

Reactant side

  • 1 carbon atom
  • 4 hydrogen atoms
  • 4 oxygen atoms

Product side

  • 1 carbon atom
  • 4 hydrogen atoms
  • 4 oxygen atoms

Every atom matches — balanced!

🧪 What is an acid?

  • Acids contain hydrogen.
  • When dissolved in water, acids release H⁺ ions (protons).
  • Common acids: HCl (hydrochloric acid), H₂SO₄ (sulfuric acid), HNO₃ (nitric acid), CH₃COOH (ethanoic/acetic acid).

📏 Strong vs weak acids

Note: "strong" and "weak" describe how completely an acid dissociates (splits into ions) in water — not how concentrated or dangerous it is.

🧂 What is a base?

  • Bases can neutralise acids to produce a salt (and usually water).
  • An alkali is simply a base that is soluble in water.

📊 The pH scale

pH measures how acidic or basic/alkaline a solution is, on a scale from 0 to 14.

0–6: Acidic
Lemon juice, vinegar, tomato, milk
8–14: Alkaline
Baking soda, soap, ammonia, drain cleaner
pH 7: Neutral — pure water sits exactly in the middle.

Indicators

Indicators are chemicals that change colour to show whether a solution is acidic, neutral, or basic — used to measure approximate pH without needing lab equipment.

⚗️ Four ways acids react

Acids react in four characteristic patterns depending on what they react with. Learn the pattern, and you can predict the products of almost any acid reaction.

1. Acid + Metal

acid + metal → salt + hydrogen
e.g. hydrochloric acid + magnesium → magnesium chloride + hydrogen
The metal ion replaces the hydrogen ions in the acid, releasing hydrogen gas.

2. Acid + Metal Oxide

acid + metal oxide → salt + water
e.g. hydrochloric acid + copper oxide → copper chloride + water
Symbol equation: CuO + H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂O
The oxide ion combines with hydrogen ions from the acid to form water.

3. Acid + Metal Hydroxide

acid + metal hydroxide → salt + water
e.g. nitric acid + calcium hydroxide → calcium nitrate + water
Symbol equation: Mg(OH)₂ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + 2H₂O
Hydroxide ions combine with hydrogen ions from the acid to form water. This is neutralisation.

4. Acid + Metal Carbonate

acid + metal carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide
e.g. hydrochloric acid + sodium carbonate → sodium chloride + carbon dioxide + water
This is the only one of the four that also produces a gas — a useful way to spot it.
Exam tip: the "salt" formed always pairs the metal from the base/carbonate with the negative ion from the acid — e.g. hydrochloric acid always produces a "-chloride" salt, sulfuric acid a "-sulfate" salt, nitric acid a "-nitrate" salt.
