⚖️ What is relative atomic mass?

Relative atomic mass (Ar) is the weighted average mass of all the isotopes of an element, compared to 1/12th the mass of a carbon-12 atom.

It takes into account both the mass of each isotope and how abundant (common) that isotope actually is in nature — which is exactly why atomic masses on the periodic table are rarely whole numbers.

🧮 The formula

Ar = (isotope₁ mass × abundance₁ + isotope₂ mass × abundance₂ + …) ÷ 100

Worked example: chlorine

Chlorine has two naturally occurring isotopes:

IsotopeRelative massAbundance
Chlorine-35 (³⁵Cl)34.96975.8%
Chlorine-37 (³⁷Cl)36.96624.2%
Ar(Cl) = (34.969 × 75.8 + 36.966 × 24.2) ÷ 100
Ar(Cl) = (2650.55 + 894.78) ÷ 100
Ar(Cl) ≈ 35.45

That's exactly the value you'll find on the periodic table for chlorine — because most chlorine atoms are chlorine-35, the average sits closer to 35 than to 37, but isn't a whole number.

📌 Key takeaways

  • Relative atomic mass is a weighted average — isotopes are so common, they pull the average toward their mass.
  • It's calculated using the mass and abundance of each isotope.
  • It explains why atomic masses on the periodic table aren't whole numbers.

⚛️ Stable vs unstable isotopes

Isotopes of the same element have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. Depending on that balance of protons to neutrons, an isotope will be:

Stable
The nucleus never undergoes nuclear decay. Most atoms around you are made of stable nuclei.
Unstable (radioisotope)
The nucleus could decay at any moment, releasing particles and/or energy. These atoms are radioactive.

💥 What is radioactive decay?

Radioactive decay is a nuclear reaction — it happens in the nucleus, not with the surrounding electrons. An unstable nucleus releases particles and/or energy to become more stable. Sometimes this changes the atom into a completely different element (transmutation); sometimes it doesn't.

Conservation still applies: just like in chemical reactions, mass and charge are conserved in nuclear decay — nothing simply vanishes. The total mass number and total atomic number must match on both sides of a decay equation.

☢️ Alpha decay

An unstable nucleus ejects an alpha particle (a helium nucleus: 2 protons + 2 neutrons)

Mass number: −4    Atomic number: −2
Because the nucleus loses 2 protons, it becomes a different element. Alpha particles are large and heavy — low penetrating power, but high ionising effect.

Worked example

Uranium-238 undergoes alpha decay:

23892U → 23490Th + 42He

Mass numbers: 238 = 234 + 4 ✓   Atomic numbers: 92 = 90 + 2 ✓

⚡ Beta (minus) decay

A neutron changes into a proton and an electron; the electron is ejected from the nucleus

Mass number: no change    Atomic number: +1
Because the nucleus gains an extra proton, it becomes a different element. Beta particles are small and fast — medium penetrating power, medium ionising effect. (This is the only type of beta decay required this year.)

Worked example

Carbon-14 undergoes beta decay:

146C → 147N + 0−1e

Mass numbers: 14 = 14 + 0 ✓   Atomic numbers: 6 = 7 + (−1) ✓

💫 Gamma decay

The nucleus releases a burst of high-energy electromagnetic radiation (a wave, not a particle)

Mass number: no change    Atomic number: no change
No protons or neutrons are gained or lost, so the atom stays the same element. Gamma rays have high penetrating power, but low ionising effect — the opposite pattern to alpha.

📊 Comparing the three types

TypeWhat's releasedChanges element?Penetrating powerIonising effect
Alpha (α)2 protons + 2 neutronsYesLowHigh
Beta (β)Fast electronYesMediumMedium
Gamma (γ)EM radiation (wave)NoHighLow
Pattern to remember: penetrating power and ionising effect run in opposite directions — what's stopped easily (alpha) damages cells the most at close range; what passes through almost anything (gamma) is the gentlest per interaction.

⏱️ What is half-life?

The rate of nuclear decay is measured using an isotope's half-life — the time it takes for half of the nuclei in a sample to decay.

Worked example: radon-222

Radon-222 alpha decays to polonium-218 with a half-life of approximately 3.82 days.

DayRadon-222 remaining
Day 0100 atoms (100%)
Day 3.82 (1 half-life)50 atoms (50%)
Day 7.64 (2 half-lives)25 atoms (25%)

After another 3.82 days, only 12–13 atoms of radon-222 would remain.

Why the range matters: short half-lives suit medical tracers (decay away quickly, safely); very long half-lives suit dating ancient rocks and fossils.

🧮 Calculating age from half-lives

Every time one half-life passes, exactly half of the remaining radioactive atoms decay:

100% → 50% → 25% → 12.5% → 6.25% → …

Worked example

A fossil contains 25% of its original carbon-14. Carbon-14's half-life is 5,730 years. How old is the fossil?

100% → 50% (1 half-life) → 25% (2 half-lives)
2 half-lives × 5,730 years = 11,460 years old
